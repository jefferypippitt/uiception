People still ask me, half joking, whether Pluto is a planet. I understand the loyalty. I grew up with nine names in a row, taped to a bedroom wall, and childhood lists feel like contracts. When the International Astronomical Union changed the rules in 2006, a lot of people heard a demotion handed down by humorless bureaucrats. It was astronomy catching up with a crowded neighborhood — and I still flinch at the empty ninth slot.

The IAU settled on three tests for a planet in our solar system. It must orbit the Sun. It must be round enough that gravity has pulled it into hydrostatic equilibrium — roughly spherical. And it must have cleared the neighborhood around its orbit.

Pluto passes the first two. It fails the third.

Clearing the neighborhood does not mean an empty sky. It means a body is gravitationally dominant along its path: it has swept up, ejected, or otherwise controlled the debris that shares its orbital zone. Earth has done this. Jupiter has done this with authority. Pluto has not. It travels through the Kuiper Belt, a broad ring of icy leftovers beyond Neptune, among peers of similar scale.

That is the heart of it. Once we found Eris, and kept finding other large Kuiper Belt objects, the old nine-planet list stopped describing the solar system we actually live in. Keep Pluto as a planet under the old habits, and you soon have a dozen or more — not a tidy classroom poster, but an honest census of small worlds at the edge.

> Pluto did not get smaller. Our map got larger.

New Horizons showed mountains of water ice, a heart-shaped plain, a thin blue haze. Dwarf planet is the category that matches where it lives: among the cold, crowded outskirts, not among the eight bodies that own their lanes.

I still miss the poster. I just stopped arguing with the belt.
