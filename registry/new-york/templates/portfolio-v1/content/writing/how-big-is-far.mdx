Ask someone how far Mars is and you will usually get a number that sounds large and means almost nothing. Millions of kilometers are hard to feel. When I talk about distance in public, I count *time for light* instead of kilometers.

Light leaves the Sun and reaches Earth in a little over eight minutes. That is already a useful unit: one astronomical unit is eight light-minutes of emptiness with a warm rock at one end.

Saturn is more than an hour away at light speed when the geometry is cooperative — longer when it is not. Voyager 1, still outbound, is measured in light-*days*. The nearest other star is measured in years. The galaxy is a structure you cannot cross in a human lifetime at any speed we know how to build.

I want the solar system to feel correctly sparse. Planets are lonely objects separated by volumes of almost nothing, held in place by gravity that does not care how empty the gaps look from here.

> Far is a clock.

Once you have that clock, conjunctions and spacecraft delay make more sense. The night sky is a set of delayed messages from objects that are still out there, doing physics, whether or not anyone is watching.
