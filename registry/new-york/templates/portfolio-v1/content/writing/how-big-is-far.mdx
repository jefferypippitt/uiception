title: How big is far

Ask someone how far Mars is and you will usually get a number that sounds large and means almost nothing. Millions of kilometers are hard to feel. When I talk about distance in public, I stop counting kilometers and start counting *time for light*.

Light leaves the Sun and reaches Earth in a little over eight minutes. That is already a useful unit: one astronomical unit is eight light-minutes of emptiness with a warm rock at one end.

Saturn is more than an hour away at light speed when the geometry is cooperative — longer when it is not. Voyager 1, still outbound, is measured in light-*days*. The nearest other star is measured in years. The galaxy is a structure you cannot cross in a human lifetime at any speed we know how to build.

I do not say this to make people feel small. I say it to make the solar system feel correctly sparse. Planets are not beads on a necklace. They are lonely objects separated by volumes of almost nothing, held in place by a gravity that does not care how empty the gaps look from here.

> Far is not a mood. It is a clock.

Once you have that clock, the rest of public astronomy gets easier. Conjunctions stop being magic. Spacecraft delay stops being a bug. And the night sky stops being a painted ceiling — it becomes a set of delayed messages from objects that are still out there, doing physics, whether or not anyone is watching.
